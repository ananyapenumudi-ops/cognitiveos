How to read this document

This is written before any code, so it is a specification and a plan, not a report. Citations in red (for example Leroy 2009) point to appendix B. Every coefficient the model uses is either tied to a source or tagged stand-in, meaning we picked a sensible value that calibration will replace. Items tagged MVP are committed for v1; v2 and Later are planned, not promised.

01 · Project overview

Model the mind, not the to-do list

Productivity apps count tasks. But two days with the same task list can feel completely different: one with long quiet blocks, one chopped up by meetings and pings. CognitiveOS models that difference. It treats a day as a sequence of demands on limited working memory and simulates how they add up.

The problem

  • Knowledge work is fragmented. In one field study, people spent on average about 11 minutes on a project before switching or being interrupted, and 57% of those stretches were interrupted Mark et al. 2005.
  • The costs are invisible. A switch leaves attention residue on the old task Leroy 2009; an alert hurts focus even if you never pick up the phone Stothart et al. 2015.
  • Tools show what you did, not why the day felt heavy, and they never let you try a different pattern before committing to it.

Our answer

  • A transparent load model: six drivers, each tied to published research, combined with weights you can see and change.
  • A simulator, not a tracker. You describe a day; nothing watches you. Then you ask "what if I batch my notifications?" and watch the curve change.
  • Exact explanations. Because the model is additive, every minute splits cleanly into its drivers. No black box to reverse-engineer.
  • Personal calibration from short NASA-TLX check-ins, with the uncertainty shown.

Who it's for

Students planning exam weeks and group projects. Developers and designers whose deep work keeps getting cut by meetings. Team leads who want to understand the cost of the interruptions they create, on their own schedule, not anyone else's. HCI and psychology learners who want to see interruption research run as a model rather than read it as a list of findings.

A user's journey

1 · Build a dayDrag tasks and meetings onto a timeline, rate how complex each is.
2 · Add the noiseAlert rate, open tasks, deadlines, planned breaks.
3 · SimulateMinute-by-minute load curve, coloured by driver.
4 · Ask whyClick a spike: which drivers, which events caused it.
5 · Try a what-ifPull a lever, compare the two days side by side.
6 · CalibrateRate real days with NASA-TLX; the weights become yours.

What it is not

Not a diagnostic tool, not a measure of anyone's ability, and not a way for employers to monitor staff. It produces an estimate under stated assumptions, and says so on every screen.

02 · The science

Six things that load a mind

Working memory holds only about four chunks at once Cowan 2001. Everything below competes for that space. For each driver: what happens, the evidence, and how CognitiveOS turns it into a number.

x₁ · intrinsic

Task complexity

Tasks whose parts must be held in mind together impose more load than tasks that can be done piece by piece Sweller 1988. Cognitive Load Theory calls this intrinsic load and separates it from load added by how work is presented Sweller et al. 1998.

How we model itEach block gets a complexity rating c from 1 (sorting email) to 5 (debugging a race condition). x₁ = c / 5 while working on it.
x₂ · switching

Switching & residue

Switching between tasks costs time even when the switch is predictable Rogers & Monsell 1995, and the cost grows with how complex the tasks are Rubinstein et al. 2001. Leaving a task unfinished keeps part of your attention on it: attention residue Leroy 2009. Resuming means re-activating the old goal Altmann & Trafton 2002.

How we model itA spike at each switch (decay τ = 3 min) plus residue proportional to the old task's complexity (τ = 12 min). stand-in τs
x₃ · alerts

Notifications

Just receiving a phone notification, without answering it, impaired performance on a sustained-attention task about as much as using the phone did Stothart et al. 2015. In a field experiment, people whose notifications were batched three times a day reported more focus and less stress Fitz et al. 2019.

How we model itEach alert adds a short spike (τ = 1.5 min). With probability p = 0.35 you act on it: a 3-minute detour that counts as a switch. Batching holds alerts until set times.
x₄ · open loops

Open loops

Unfinished goals keep intruding on unrelated work. Making a specific plan for the goal, without finishing it, removed that interference Masicampo & Baumeister 2011.

How we model itBackground load that saturates with the number of open tasks n: 1 − e−n/4. Halved when each task has a written next step.
x₅ · time

Deadline pressure

Temporal demand is one of the six dimensions of the NASA-TLX workload scale Hart & Staveland 1988. Interrupted people finished tasks faster but reported more stress, frustration, time pressure and effort Mark et al. 2008.

How we model itWork left ÷ time left for each deadline, ramping in over the last 4 hours. The tightest deadline wins.
x₆ · fatigue

Fatigue

Mental fatigue builds with time on a demanding task and reduces engagement Boksem & Tops 2008. Brief, occasional breaks from a task prevented the usual drop in vigilance Ariga & Lleras 2011.

How we model itAn accumulator that grows faster under high load and recovers during breaks (τ = 14 min). It lags, so a heavy morning shows up after lunch.

03 · The model

One number, six honest parts

Load at minute t is a weighted sum of the six drivers. Simple on purpose: a sum can be explained exactly, tested for sensible behaviour, and fitted to a person with very little data.

// load at minute t, from the six drivers in section 02
L(t) = w₁·complexity + w₂·switching + w₃·alerts + w₄·openLoops + w₅·deadline + w₆·fatigue
switching(t) = Σswitches s e−(t−s)/3 + 0.75 · Σs (cprev/5) · e−(t−s)/12
fatigue(t+1) = F + (0.4 + L)(1 − F)/260 while working · F · e−1/14 during breaks
// default weights (stand-ins until calibration): w = (0.34, 0.30, 0.16, 0.14, 0.14, 0.22)

Reading the sample day

Mean load is {{BASE_MEAN}} and {{BASE_OVER}} minutes sit above 0.85. Switching and residue account for {{BASE_SWITCH}}% of all load, as much as the work itself: the late-morning spikes are switches between code and the report, not hard tasks.

Zones, stated carefully

We shade four bands: under-loaded (< 0.3), focus band, strained (≥ 0.65), overloaded (≥ 0.85). They echo the inverted-U idea that performance peaks at moderate arousal Yerkes & Dodson 1908, but that "law" has been restated so many ways that it can't be treated as one Teigen 1994. So the bands are a display convention that calibration moves, not a claim.

Calibration

After a real block of work, a 30-second check-in asks the six raw NASA-TLX questions Hart 2006. From 8 check-ins on, we fit personal weights by non-negative ridge regression, pulled towards the defaults, and show a bootstrap interval around every curve. Fewer check-ins: defaults, labelled as such.

04 · What-if experiments

Try the day before you live it

The point of a simulator is the counterfactual. Same tasks, same total work, a different arrangement. We ran the prototype on the sample day with three levers from the research, alone and together. These numbers come from stand-in parameters on an invented day: they show the simulator moves in the direction the studies predict, not real effect sizes.

Batching wins alone

In this model an acted-on alert becomes a switch, so batching removes dozens of small switches. That's why it beats the other two levers on its own. Whether that holds for a real person is what calibration finds out.

Levers stack

Together the three levers do more than any one: long morning blocks leave fewer switches for alerts to multiply. The What-If Lab shows each lever's share of the gain, so the user knows which habit is worth keeping.

Too calm is a result too

The combined day moves a lot of time into the under-loaded band. CognitiveOS reports that instead of treating lower as always better. An under-loaded afternoon is a cue to schedule the hard task there.

Where the "AI" is, honestly

The core is an interpretable model, not a neural network, because a load estimate you can't explain is worse than none. The AI parts sit around it: learning personal weights from check-ins, searching for better schedules, and narrating results. None of them can change what the model says happened.

06 · Scenario library

Six people, six levers

Each scenario is a ready-made day plus one lever to try. The "expect" line is what the model should show if it's built right; these double as end-to-end tests.

S1 · Student

Exam week

Four subjects in one day, phone face-up, about 30 alerts an hour from group chats, a lab report due at 23:59.

Lever Batch alerts to the top of each hour. Expect switching share falls; deadline pressure unchanged.
S2 · Developer

The chopped-up maker

Four 30-minute meetings spread evenly; no gap longer than 70 minutes for complexity-5 work.

Lever Cluster meetings after lunch. Expect fewer strained minutes in the morning; a dip in the meeting block.
S3 · Engineer on call

Pager day

A high alert rate where most alerts must be acted on (p = 0.8), on top of a planned feature task.

Lever Hand the pager over for a 2-hour block. Expect one clean focus window; the model shows what the hand-over buys.
S4 · Team member

The juggler

Nine open tasks across three group projects, none finished today, each touched briefly.

Lever Write a next step for every open task. Expect open-loop load halves; switching unchanged.
S5 · Researcher

The marathon

One complexity-4 task for five hours straight, few alerts, no breaks.

Lever A 5-minute break every 50 minutes. Expect fatigue stays lower all afternoon at the cost of 25 minutes of work.
S6 · Freelancer

Deadline crunch

Three client projects, two due tomorrow, more work left than hours in the day.

Lever Drop or renegotiate one project. Expect deadline pressure and open loops both fall; shows the cost of over-committing.

08 · Tech stack

Browser-first, local-first

Everything runs on the user's device. A day is a small JSON document and a simulation is a few hundred thousand arithmetic operations, so there's no reason to send either to a server. The only network call is the optional AI narrator.

LayerChoiceWhy
UIReact 19 · TypeScript · ViteSame toolchain as SignalSafe, so components, lint and CI setup carry over.
StateZustandSmall store for the day being edited, the active what-if and the selected minute.
Timeline editingdnd-kitAccessible drag and drop with keyboard support, needed for snapping blocks.
ChartsD3 scales & shapes → React SVGStacked areas, donuts and overlays with full control over the design language; SVG exports cleanly.
EnginePure TypeScript in a Web Worker (Comlink)No DOM, no dates, no randomness except a seeded PRNG: easy to test and identical on every machine.
SchemasZodOne schema validates days, scenarios and imported files, and generates the TypeScript types.
StorageIndexedDB via DexieDays, check-ins and fitted weights stay in the browser. Export and import as JSON.
FittingSmall NNLS ridge solver in TSSix weights, a few dozen check-ins: no ML library needed, and the fit is fully inspectable.
AI narratorClaude API (Haiku 4.5) via a serverless proxyOpt-in. Only the attribution summary is sent, never the raw day. The proxy keeps the API key off the client.
TestingVitest · fast-check · PlaywrightUnit and property tests for the engine; scenario tests from section 06; end-to-end tests for the main flows.
DeliveryGitHub Actions · VercelTests and type checks on every push; preview deploy per pull request.

What we deliberately skip

No accounts, no backend database, no analytics SDK, no browser extension that watches activity. Each would make the tool more powerful and less trustworthy.

Performance budget

A full 16-hour day (960 ticks) simulates in under 20 ms, so dragging a block re-runs the model live. Schedule Search runs thousands of these in the worker.

Data model

type Block    = { from: number; to: number; task: string; kind: 'work' | 'meeting' | 'break'; c: 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 }
type Day      = { minutes: number; blocks: Block[]; alertsPerHour: number; actOnAlerts: number;
                batchAt: number[] | null; openTasks: { name: string; planned: boolean }[];
                deadlines: { at: number; workLeft: number }[]; seed: number }
type Tick     = { t: number; load: number; parts: Record<Driver, number>; events: Event[] }
type Lever    = (day: Day) => Day            // batch alerts, write plans, cluster meetings, add breaks…
type CheckIn  = { blockId: string; tlx: [number, number, number, number, number, number]; at: string }
type Param    = { value: number; source: string; standIn: boolean; note: string }

The tick loop

For each minute: find the active block, register switches and delivered alerts as events, evaluate the six drivers, weight and sum them, then advance fatigue. Events are kept so the "Why?" card can name the cause, not just the share.

Levers are pure functions

A what-if never edits the user's day. It applies a list of Lever transforms to a copy, so variants can be combined, reordered and attributed one lever at a time.

10 · Principles

Built to be trusted

The weakest version of this project is a to-do app with a made-up score. These rules are what keep it from becoming that.

1 · Every number has a source

Each parameter lives in params.ts with its value, citation, a stand-in flag and a note. The Model Card renders that file directly, so the docs can't drift from the code.

2 · Explanations are exact

Load is a sum, so driver shares are exact arithmetic, not an approximation like SHAP. The "Why?" card can't contradict the model because it is the model.

3 · Uncertainty is shown

Default weights are labelled as defaults. Calibrated curves carry a bootstrap band. The check-in screen shows where the model was wrong.

4 · A simulator, not a diagnosis

No clinical words (burnout, ADHD, stress disorder), no scores about a person's ability, and nothing to hand to an employer. It's a planning aid for your own day.

5 · Private by construction

No accounts, no tracking, no server storage. The AI narrator is off by default; when on, it sees only the six-number summary, never task names.

6 · Lower isn't always better

Under-load is reported as clearly as overload. The goal is time in the focus band, not an empty calendar.

Properties the engine must always satisfy

PropertyTest (fast-check, random valid days)
DeterministicSame day + same seed → identical trace, bit for bit.
More alerts never helpAdding an alert never lowers load at any minute.
Breaks never add fatigueTurning any work minute into a break never raises fatigue later.
Plans never hurtMarking an open task as planned never raises open-loop load.
Shares add upAt every minute the six parts sum to the total within 1e-9.
BoundedEvery driver stays in [0, 1.2]; load is finite for days up to 24 h.

11 · Build plan

Six weeks, one loop at a time

Each week ends with something that runs. Week 2 already has a working headless simulator; everything after it is ways to see, change and trust it.

WEEK 1 · MODEL & RESEARCH

Parameters with sources

Read the 19 sources properly; write params.ts with every value, citation and stand-in flag. Port the prototype from this doc to TypeScript. Write the six scenario days as JSON.

Deterministic simulator in a worker

Tick loop, seeded alert generator, six drivers, fatigue, event log. Property tests from section 10. Scenario "expect" lines as tests.

12 · Future features

What could come next

Planned in rough order of value. Anything that touches more personal data stays opt-in and local.

Schedule Search v2

Given fixed meetings and movable work, search arrangements for the fewest overloaded minutes and the most focus-band time on hard tasks. Simulated annealing over block orderings, all in the worker, with the winning schedule explained lever by lever.

Personal calibration v2

Weights fitted from check-ins, with intervals and a "how much has this changed my model" view. Later: hierarchical priors so a new user starts from what others' data suggests, without sharing anyone's data.

AI Narrator v2

A weekly plain-language summary ("your Tuesdays are heavy because of three switches before 11:00"), generated from attribution data and rejected automatically if any number doesn't match it.

Calendar import Later

Read an .ics file or a calendar export to build tomorrow's day in one step, then ask the user to rate complexity, since a calendar can't know that.

A small validation study Later

With consent, a handful of volunteers rate real blocks for two weeks. Compare default and calibrated predictions against their TLX ratings, report error honestly, and publish the protocol and results.

Physiological signals Research

Heart-rate variability from a wearable as an optional extra input for calibration. Kept as research until it is clear it adds signal and not just noise and privacy risk.

Ruled out on purpose

Team dashboards that rank people, automatic activity tracking, and employer-facing reports. They would turn a tool for understanding your own day into a tool for watching other people's.

Glossary & design language

TermMeaning in this project
Cognitive loadThe demand a situation places on limited working memory. Here: the model's output L(t), an estimate, not a measurement.
Intrinsic loadLoad from the task itself: how many elements must be held in mind at once Sweller et al. 1998.
Switch costThe extra time and errors that come with changing tasks, even when the change is expected Monsell 2003.
Attention residueThoughts about a previous, unfinished task that carry into the next one Leroy 2009.
Working sphereA unit of work with a shared goal, used in field studies of fragmented work Mark et al. 2005.
Open loopA task started and not finished, which keeps drawing attention until it is done or planned.
NASA-TLXA six-question workload scale: mental, physical and temporal demand, performance, effort, frustration. "Raw" TLX averages the six without weighting Hart 2006.
LeverA what-if change to a day, written as a pure function from one day to another.
Stand-inA parameter value we chose because the literature doesn't give a usable number at a one-minute scale. Flagged everywhere it appears.
Focus bandThe middle zone, 0.3 to 0.65 by default. A display convention, moved by calibration.

Where it comes from

Three references: the Inside Out infographic (one saturated colour per feeling, thought orbs, donut charts), the "Standing on the shoulders of giants" poster (layered flat profile heads, leader-line labels) and a self-confidence slide deck (warm beige paper, tall condensed capitals, black pill labels).
